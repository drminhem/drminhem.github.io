Food and nutrition

Can diet help prevent cancer?

A healthy eating pattern can help lower the risk of some cancers, but no diet guarantees prevention. Cancer has many causes; a diagnosis does not mean someone ate badly. Aim for practical habits you can maintain, rather than a perfect diet.

Build meals around varied plant foods

Include vegetables, whole fruit, beans or lentils, and whole grains regularly. These foods provide fiber and other nutrients. Familiar choices such as lentil soup, beans, bulgur and wholegrain bread can fit this pattern; no expensive special food is required.

The overall pattern matters more than adding one supposed superfood. Choose changes that fit your appetite, budget and household meals.

Limit processed meat, red meat and alcohol

Eat little, if any, processed meat such as sausages and cured deli meats. Limit red meat, including beef and lamb, and choose beans, fish or poultry more often. Evidence linking red and processed meat to cancer is strongest for colorectal cancer.

For cancer prevention, avoiding alcohol is best. Choose water or unsweetened drinks more often than sugary drinks, and limit foods that are high in added sugar and provide few nutrients.

Do not rely on prevention supplements

Vitamin, antioxidant or herbal supplements are not recommended as a way to prevent cancer. Some high-dose supplements can cause harm. A supplement prescribed for a specific deficiency is a different matter: discuss the reason and dose with your clinician.

Healthy eating is one part of risk reduction, alongside avoiding tobacco, being active and receiving appropriate screening. It does not replace screening or assessment of a new symptom.

Adapt the advice to your situation

During cancer treatment, poor appetite or weight loss may make additional calories and protein the priority. Do not apply restrictive prevention diets to that situation without advice. After treatment, ask how to move towards a sustainable eating pattern that fits your recovery and any ongoing digestive problems.

Questions to ask at your consultation

  • Which dietary changes would be most useful for me?
  • Do any health conditions change this advice?
  • What screening is appropriate for my age and personal or family history?
