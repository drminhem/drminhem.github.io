More than a PSA number

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is a blood marker used in assessment and follow-up. A PSA result alone does not describe the cancer; biopsy grade and extent of disease also matter.

How I can help

A treatment plan tailored to your cancer, test results, overall health and preferences, informed by current evidence.

How do the biopsy and PSA fit together?

Explain the biopsy's Gleason score or Grade Group, which describes cell appearance, alongside PSA trends and imaging.

Is monitoring or treatment appropriate?

Discuss whether structured active surveillance is appropriate or treatment is indicated, based on the cancer, overall health and preferences. Surveillance involves planned monitoring, not simply ignoring the diagnosis.

Why might hormone treatment be proposed?

If hormone treatment is proposed, clarify its purpose, duration and possible effects on energy, sexual function and bone health. A second opinion can help prepare questions for your treating team.

Reports to bring, if available

Bring the reports you already have. This list does not mean every test is needed.

  • Existing biopsy report with Gleason score or Grade Group.
  • Dated PSA blood-test results, including earlier measurements.
  • Any prostate MRI or staging scan reports and images.
  • Existing treatment summaries, including hormone medicines and dates.

Please also bring your medication list, previous treatment records, and questions.

Questions about prostate cancer decisions

  • How do the biopsy grade, PSA trend and scans fit together?
  • If hormone treatment is proposed, why is it needed and for how long?
  • Is active surveillance appropriate for my cancer, and what would monitoring involve?
